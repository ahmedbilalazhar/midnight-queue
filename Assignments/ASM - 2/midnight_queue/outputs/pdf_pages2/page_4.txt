F. The Mathematical Case
M1 — Insertion Sort. Let I be the number of inversions. Every inversion moves the key one slot left, so moves are I+(n−1). Comparisons are I+(n−1)−z, where z≤n−1 is the
number of keys that reach the left end without a failing comparison; hence C=Θ(n+I). Best I=0 → Θ(n); average I=n(n−1)/4 and worst I=n(n−1)/2 → Θ(n²). On C5, I = 3,007,
325,988, 34,333,390 and 272,954,546 for n=1k, 10k, 100k and full. The 1% displacement does not keep I linear.
M2 — Merge Sort. T(n)=2T(n/2)+Θ(n). Iteration: level j has 2^j nodes of size n/2^j and costs c n until subproblems are constant, over log2 n levels → Θ(n log n). Master
theorem: a=2, b=2, f(n)=Θ(n)=Θ(n^{log_b a}), case 2 → Θ(n log n).
M3 — Quick Sort. (a) Worst: T(n)=T(n−1)+Θ(n). Iterating gives c[n+(n−1)+…+1]=Θ(n²). (b) Best: T(n)=2T(n/2)+Θ(n); the recursion tree has n per level and log2 n levels →
Θ(n log n). (c) 1:9 split: T(n)=T(n/10)+T(9n/10)+n. The slow branch has depth log_{10/9}n; each level costs at most n before the leaves, so T(n)=Θ(n log n). A 1:9 split is
unbalanced but not degenerate.
M4 — Heap Sort. A node of height h has at most ceil(n/2^{h+1}) nodes at that height and sifts in O(h). Build-heap is Σ_h O(nh/2^h)=O(n). Each of n−1 extractions sifts O(log
n), so total Θ(n log n); the heap is in place.
M5 — Top-K. (a) Full sort then prefix: Θ(n log n), independent of K. (b) Size-K heap: Θ(n log K) offers plus Θ(K log K) final publication, space Θ(K). (c) Bounded buffer: worst
O(nK); if A records are accepted, O(n+A(log K+K)). For a random stream A≈K ln(n/K), so shifting—not binary search—is the bottleneck when K grows. The asymptotic
heap/full-sort crossover is K=Θ(n), not K=Θ(n/10). At n=100,000 C2 the heap beats the full sort at every measured K from 10 to 10,000; at K=10,000 it takes 0.498 s against
1.20 s for the full sort. At full size and K=n/10=28,480 it takes 1.29 s against 3.56 s. The log predicted a crossover near K=10,000; that prediction was wrong. For K=αn, heap
and full sort break even when α log2 n+(1+α)log2 α=0; at α=0.1 this needs n≈2^36.5≈10^11, far beyond this dataset, so full sort becomes competitive only when K is close to
n. The measured grid is in results.csv.

M6 — Naive Quick Sort on C6. Lomuto sends every equal band left and leaves the pivot at the right end of its equal block. A one-band subarray of size m therefore costs
(m−1)+(m−2)+…+1 = m(m−1)/2 comparisons; cross-band partitions add O(nd). With the actual groups this predicts 16.00M at 10k, 1.59B at 100k and 12.93B at full size. The
measured 10k count is 16,023,019, only 0.17% above the group formula (the element-level model gives 16,012,977, +0.11%); at 1k the formula is 156,718 versus 158,620
measured (+1.2%). The 100k/full cells are DNF, as predicted. Three-way partitioning puts all equals in one pass, which is why the hardened variant survives.
M7 — Live feed. (a) Re-rank: Σ_{j=1}^w Θ((jn/w) log(jn/w)) = Θ(nw log n). (b) Heap: Θ(n log K + wK log K). (c) Window merge: Θ(n log(n/w) + wK). Comparing (b) and (c), the
dominant terms give n log(n/w)≈n log K, hence w≈n/K: fewer, larger windows favor merge, while many small windows favor the heap. Re-rank carries an extra factor w and is
dominated by the heap for every w≥1 when K<n.
M8 — Space. Insertion O(1); Merge O(n); Quick O(log n) stack with smaller-side-first; Heap O(1); size-K heap O(K); bounded buffer O(K); live re-rank O(n); live heap O(K);
live merge O(n/w + K).

H. Recommendation to the Platform Lead
1. Overnight full-day batch: ship Heap Sort. Across the five total-order conditions at full size, the worst measured times are Merge Sort 20.08 s (C1); Heap Sort 9.68 s (C2);
Hardened Quick Sort 1.39 min (C4). Heap Sort has the lowest worst case. It is in-place O(1) and Θ(n log n) in the worst case; it is unstable, but txn_id makes the triage key
total, so stability is not needed. Merge Sort wins the two random/adversarial orders C2 (7.06 s) and C4 (5.83 s); if the platform knows the batch is always shuffled or
reverse-ranked, ship Merge Sort instead. The recommendation would be wrong if the batch were already pre-ranked (C3), where insertion sort takes only 0.642 s.
2. Shift queue of K=52: ship the size-K heap. The buffer does win the 100,000-record K=52 corner: 0.032 s against 0.035 s for the heap on C2 (and similarly on C1). That 4
ms edge does not survive scale or larger K. At full size K=52 the heap takes 0.059 s against 0.079 s; at K=2,068 it takes 0.134 s against 1.24 s; at K=n/10 it takes 1.29 s
against 2.69 min. Over the 28 measured cells the heap is at most 1.12× slower than the best method, while the buffer is up to 140.9× slower and the full sort up to 60.0×
slower. The heap is the robust choice because it wins 25 cells and never loses by much, not because it wins every cell. The assumption is K much smaller than n and
immutable scores after arrival. The choice would be wrong if K were always K=52 at n≈100,000, where the buffer is a few milliseconds faster, or if late corrections could
change an old record's score, which needs an update path or periodic rebuild. Operationally the K=52 queue contains 0 fraud(s); reaching half the frauds needs K≈2,068, and
the heap is still the appropriate structure there.
3. Live refresh every window: ship the maintained size-K heap. Measured totals — 60-min: A 1.34 min, B 0.084 s, C 2.32 s; 10-min: A DNF, B 0.169 s, C 2.06 s. Winner:
60-min: B (heap); 10-min: B (heap). The 10-minute re-rank is a DNF at the 300 s watchdog, not a missing measurement. It offers each new record once, never re-ranks
history, and publishes the committed queue after every window. The asymptotic switch is at w≈n/K: with n=284,807 and K=52 that is about 5,477 windows (a window of ~52
records). Both tested schedules are far below it, so the heap is expected to win; it would be wrong for extremely small windows, where merge's tiny window sort plus K-merge
can beat a per-record sift, or if scores can be revised after arrival, where the heap needs an update path.
